\begin{abstract}
  Proportional representation is usually evaluated party by party, by comparing
  each party's share of seats with its share of votes. Yet fragmented
  legislatures are governed through combinations of parties, so small
  party-level deviations can become consequential when parties are combined.
  Brazil provides a useful setting because the Chamber of Deputies is assembled
  from twenty-seven separate district elections. Several institutional steps
  separate national votes from final party seat totals, and no national
  compensatory allocation reconciles them. I examine which politically
  meaningful sets of parties hold a legislative majority with less than half of
  all valid federal-deputy votes nationwide, and how within-district allocation
  and the distribution of support contribute to these coalition
  inversions. Using the 2014, 2018, and 2022 elections, I compare distinct
  election-year cabinet party sets with potential coalitions restricted by
  ideological proximity. Cabinet inversions appear following the 2014 and 2022
  elections, but not the 2018 election; inversions among minimal connected
  winning coalitions appear in all three. In 2022, a seven-party bloc of
  ideologically adjacent parties combined 45.26 percent of the vote with 50.29
  percent of the seats. Coalition formation turns otherwise continuous vote-seat
  deviations into a discontinuous political consequence when their accumulation
  carries a party set across the legislative majority threshold.

\section{Introduction}

Under proportional representation, vote--seat translation is usually assessed
party by party, by comparing each party's vote and seat shares \parencite{kaminski26_propor_repres_primer}. In
fragmented legislatures, however, governing and legislative majorities are
assembled from several parties. Combining parties can make individually modest
deviations consequential. Positive and negative deviations may cancel or
reinforce one another, allowing a party set to cross the legislative majority
threshold before its combined vote share reaches one half. I refer to this
configuration as a coalition inversion: a party set holds a seat majority while
receiving less than half of all valid national legislative votes.
 


\textcite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres} documents empirical cases of coalition inversions\footnote{ I use
  \emph{coalition inversion} as shorthand for Miller's \emph{coalitionwise
    election inversion}.} in Danish government formation. \textcite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres} establishes the
more general result that inversions remain possible even when seats are
allocated in a single national constituency, without an explicit seat threshold,
and by a highly proportional formula. These works settle the existence question.
They do not show how coalition inversions appear when national party seat totals
are themselves assembled from many district elections.

Brazil provides such a setting. The Chamber of Deputies is not formed by
applying one proportional allocation to national party votes. Its seats are
allocated separately across the twenty-six states and the Federal District.
District magnitudes and electoral weights vary, electoral rules and the units
over which votes were pooled changed across the three elections, and party
support is distributed unevenly across the country. Several institutional and
geographical mediations therefore stand between a party's national vote share
and its final national seat share \parencite{bochsler2026winners}.  


Party-level deviations emerge from twenty-seven district-level translations
operating under heterogeneous conditions, rather than only from rounding a
single national allocation. The organizing question is therefore: when do these
deviations combine so that a politically meaningful set of parties holds a
legislative majority without a national vote majority? The contribution is to
show which governing combinations and ideologically structured majorities
benefit from these deviations, and how their advantages reflect coalition
composition, within-district allocation, and the distribution of
electoral support.

Coalition inversion becomes more politically informative once the combinations
of parties under consideration are given substantive structure. I examine the
phenomenon in two domains that capture distinct ways parties combine in
parliamentary politics. The first is realized cabinet composition. Changes in
ministerial participation generate successive cabinet party sets during a
legislative term; for each, I compare the Chamber seats held by its members with
their combined vote share in the election that produced the legislature.

The second domain examines inversions in the ideological structure of the party
system. Following \textcite{axelrod1970conflict}, I identify minimal connected
winning coalitions: contiguous blocs in the election-year left--right order that
hold a seat majority and lose it if narrowed. A one-gap sensitivity permits one
represented party inside the span to be omitted. Taken together, the two domains
locate coalition inversions in realized governing combinations and in
ideologically structured potential majorities. For both, I examine how
coalition-level departures from proportionality reflect vote-to-seat translation
within districts and differences in electoral weight across districts.

Inversions appear in both coalition domains, but their distribution differs
across elections. Two of the 35 distinct cabinet party sets invert: one observed for five days
under Dilma Rousseff following the 2014 election and one observed during the first 255
days of Lula III following the 2022 election. No cabinet party set linked to
the 2018 election inverts. Minimal connected
winning inversions, by contrast, appear in all three elections. The 2022
cases include a compact right-side bloc with 45.26 percent of the vote and
258 seats. Within-district and between-district components reinforce one
another in some inversions and offset one another in others.

\section{Brazil's multidistrict open-list proportional system}
\label{sec:brazil-system}

Coalition majorities are formed from national party seat totals, but those
totals are not produced by a national allocation. In Brazil, elections to the
Chamber of Deputies take place separately in the twenty-six states and the
Federal District, each of which forms an electoral district. The 513 seats are
allocated within these twenty-seven districts and then summed nationally. The
Federal District is simply an eight-seat district, and no compensatory tier
subsequently adjusts the resulting party totals, unlike compensatory mixed
systems, where a second allocation stage can correct constituency-level
disproportionality \parencite{FlisGrofmanKaminski2025Introduction, salamon2026mixed}.

District magnitude varies considerably across states, from eight to seventy
seats. Eleven districts elect only eight deputies, the median magnitude is ten,
the mean nineteen, and S\~ao Paulo elects seventy. These differences imply
substantial variation in the effective conditions of representation across
districts: winning a seat is generally more demanding where fewer seats are
available. Because each district is allocated independently, the resulting
advantages and shortfalls carry into national party seat totals.

Within each district, voters encounter an open-list election. They may enter the
identifying number of an individual candidate or cast a vote for a party label.
A candidate vote performs two functions at once. It contributes to the aggregate
vote of the party, electoral coalition, or federation through which the
candidate competes, and it raises that candidate's position within the relevant
open list. A party-label vote contributes to the first calculation but not to
the ranking among candidates. That is, votes first determine how many seats an
electoral list receives. Nominal votes then determine which candidates within
that list take the seats, subject to the individual eligibility requirements in
force for the election. A candidate can therefore benefit from votes cast for
copartisans or electoral partners, but cannot ordinarily occupy a seat unless
the list to which the candidate belongs wins one \parencite{nicolau2006openlist}.

Valid candidate and party-label votes are first aggregated for the electoral
unit in force in each district, whether a party, proportional coalition, or
federation. The electoral quotient is obtained from the total valid vote and the
number of seats available in the district, using the statutory rounding rule.
Each electoral unit then receives an initial allocation of seats according to
how many times its vote total reaches that quotient, subject to the
candidate-eligibility requirements in force for the election. Any seats left
unfilled are distributed one at a time by the Jefferson--D'Hondt
highest-averages procedure, with each unit's priority declining as it
accumulates seats. Eligibility to compete for these remaining seats varies
across election years, as described below \parencite{nicolauSchmitt1995}.

Eligibility rules changed across elections. In 2014, there was no fixed
candidate threshold, but only lists reaching the electoral quotient could
contest remainders. In 2018, candidates ordinarily needed 10 percent of the
quotient; all lists could contest remainders while an eligible candidate
remained, after which any still-unfilled seats were assigned by highest averages
without that nominal-vote minimum. In 2022, the 10 percent candidate threshold
remained for initial seats. For remainder seats, lists first needed 80 percent
of the quotient and candidates 20 percent; any seats still unfilled were then
assigned by highest averages among lists meeting the 80 percent requirement,
without the 20 percent candidate threshold.

Proportional electoral coalitions, or \emph{coligações proporcionais}, were
part of the 2014 and 2018 elections. They were formed separately within each
electoral district, so a party could enter different proportional arrangements
in different states. These were therefore not national alliances whose
composition automatically carried across all twenty-seven contests. Member
parties operated as a joint open list when votes were converted into seats:
candidate and party-label votes credited to any member entered the coalition's
district total, seats were assigned to the coalition as a whole, and the
highest nominal vote-getters across the combined list occupied them. A candidate
from one party could therefore be elected partly because votes cast for
candidates or labels of another coalition member raised the joint total
\parencite{nicolau2006openlist}.

This resembles list apparentement because parties pool votes before seats are
settled, but the Brazilian arrangement differs from the two-stage system studied
by \textcite{pukelsheimLeutgaeb2013apparentements}. There, alliance seats are
subsequently apportioned among component lists. In Brazil, a single joint open
list selected winners by candidate votes, so gains to particular members depended
on the distribution of nominal votes rather than on a fixed secondary
apportionment rule.


Constitutional reform in 2017 abolished proportional electoral coalitions
beginning with the 2020 elections. Party federations were introduced by
legislation in 2021 as a new form of multiparty organization for proportional
elections. Three federations competed in the 2022 general election:
PT--PCdoB--PV, PSDB--CIDADANIA, and PSOL--REDE. Like the former
\emph{coligações}, federations pooled their members' votes in a common open
list, so constituent-party seat totals resulted from competition within the
pooled list rather than from independent proportional allocations. Unlike the
former electoral coalitions, federations operate nationally and in principle
persist for at least four years. They have some common congressional procedures,
including leadership and committee-allocation arrangements, but do not merge
their constituent parties, which retain separate identities and organizational
autonomy \parencite{fischMesquita2022}.



Several mediations therefore stand between a party's national vote share and its national seat share. Within each district, finite magnitude, the quotient and highest-averages calculations, legal eligibility thresholds, and the number and relative strength of competing electoral units shape the conversion of votes into seats. Electoral pooling adds a further mediation by changing both the vote total on which seats are allocated and, through the common candidate list, the eventual distribution of those seats among member parties. These arrangements varied across districts in 2014 and 2018 and became nationally fixed through federations in 2022.

The national seat distribution then aggregates twenty-seven independently determined district outcomes. Chamber seats are apportioned by population, but the constitutional floor of eight and ceiling of seventy prevent exact population-to-seat equality across states \parencite{samuelsSnyder2001value}. Turnout and valid-vote rates also differ across elections and districts, so a seat can correspond to different numbers of valid votes in different states. Because parties distribute their support unevenly across the country, national vote shares can therefore be assembled from geographically different configurations with different representational consequences. Electoral geography interacts with district seat weights and local competitive conditions before the resulting party seats are summed nationally. The resulting disproportionality has multiple sources: allocation rules, district magnitude, malapportionment and electoral weight, electoral geography, and party competition constitute distinct channels through which party vote and seat shares can diverge \parencite{bochsler2026winners}.

This layered translation occurs in an unusually fragmented party environment. Measured by the Laakso--Taagepera index, the effective numbers of electoral parties were 14.11 in 2014, 18.02 in 2018, and 12.34 in 2022; the corresponding effective numbers of parliamentary parties were 13.33, 16.41, and 9.91 \parencite{laakso1979effective}. The exact number of allocation units was not identical to the number of party labels, especially when district coalitions or national federations pooled votes. Even so, each election produced a Chamber in which legislative majorities had to be assembled across numerous partisan units.

The national party seat totals produced by this process enter a presidential
system in which governing capacity ordinarily depends on multiparty
arrangements. Brazilian coalitional presidentialism links the presidency to
combinations of parties across cabinet and Congress: ministries are distributed
among several parties, while executive--legislative coordination is organized
through agreements among them
\parencite{abranches1988presidencialismo,figueiredo2000presidential}.
The Chamber assembled from twenty-seven district elections therefore supplies
the parliamentary resources from which governing coalitions are constructed.


The realized party seat totals incorporate the effects of rounding, highest
averages, district magnitude, eligibility requirements, electoral pooling,
apportionment, turnout, valid-vote variation, electoral geography, and
district-level competition. Comparing those totals with national proportional
quotas identifies the positive and negative representational deviations carried
by each party. That is, national proportionality provides a common baseline
against which to assess how Brazil’s layered vote-to-seat process generates
representational deviations and when those deviations become majority-changing
as parties are recombined into coalitions. If \(v_i\) is the national valid vote
total credited to party \(i\) and \(V\) is the corresponding total for all
parties, including those that win no seats, the party's reference quota is
\(513v_i/V\) seats. We use all valid federal-deputy votes, including votes cast
for parties that win no seats. Restricting the denominator to seat-winning
parties would instead measure proportionality conditional on electoral
inclusion. Here, exclusion remains part of the vote-to-seat transformation,
since votes excluded from representation can affect the allocation of seats
among represented parties
\parencite{kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system}.

\section{Coalition inversions, domains, and measurement}
\label{sec:mea}


Party seat shares need not match their national vote shares exactly. When
parties are combined, these departures from proportionality may offset or
reinforce one another. A coalition inversion occurs when their combined effect
is sufficient for a party set to reach the Chamber's 257-seat majority threshold
while still receiving less than half of the national vote.

To give this coalition-level phenomenon political content, I examine it in two
domains that capture distinct forms of party combination. Observed cabinets
identify realized governing combinations, showing when inversion appears in the
composition of government. Ideological connectedness identifies potential
parliamentary majorities structured by proximity in the party system. The two
domains therefore place the same representational phenomenon in realized
government composition and in the ideological structure of majority formation.

Cabinet coalitions are substantively meaningful because Brazilian presidents
ordinarily govern through multiparty arrangements. Cabinet participation records
a party combination that was not merely arithmetically possible but
institutionally realized in executive government. The relevant representational
question is, thus, whether the members of the party set holding ministries
collectively possessed a Chamber majority despite receiving less than half of
the valid federal-deputy vote in the election that produced that legislature.
Cabinet membership changes during a legislative term while the underlying
election result remains fixed. As parties enter or leave, the governing
combination can move into or out of inversion without any new electoral
allocation. The cabinet sequence therefore shows whether, and for how long,
realized governing sets hold a Chamber majority without a vote majority.
Observed cabinets cover only a small share of possible parliamentary majorities,
so I also examine potential coalitions structured by ideological proximity.
Following \textcite{axelrod1970conflict}, I use minimal connected winning coalitions: contiguous intervals
in the election-year ideological order that hold at least 257 seats and lose
that majority when narrowed.


\subsection{Measurement }

Let \(P\) denote the set of all parties receiving valid votes in a
federal-deputy election, including those that win no seats. For each party \(i\in P\),
let \(v_i\) be its national total of valid candidate and party-label
votes and \(s_i\) its Chamber seats. With \(V=\sum_{i\in P}v_i\) denoting
the total valid federal-deputy vote and \(S=513\), exact national
proportionality assigns party \(i\) the quota
\[
q_i=S\frac{v_i}{V}.
\]

The party seat differential is $d_i=s_i-q_i$; positive values denote
more seats than the national quota and negative values fewer. Because seats and
quotas each sum to $S$, party differentials sum to zero. For coalition
$C\subseteq P$,
\[
v_C=\sum_{i\in C}v_i,\quad s_C=\sum_{i\in C}s_i,\quad
q_C=S\frac{v_C}{V},\quad d_C=s_C-q_C=\sum_{i\in C}d_i.
\]

The coalition's departure from exact national proportionality is produced by
adding the positive and negative differentials of its member parties. A party
with a negative contribution can belong to an inverted coalition, provided that
the positive contributions elsewhere in the set more than offset it.

A coalition inversion occurs when the party set receives less than half of the
national valid federal-deputy vote but controls at least 257 Chamber seats:
\(\frac{v_C}{V}<\frac{1}{2}\) and \(s_C\geq257.\)
For a vote-minority coalition, the seat-majority condition is
$d_C\geq257-q_C$: its accumulated differential must bridge the distance from
its national proportional quota to 257 seats. Because $C$ and its complement
partition both votes and seats, an inversion simultaneously gives the complement
a vote majority and at most 256 seats. Throughout, $V$ includes all valid
federal-deputy votes, including votes for parties winning no seats.


Representation ratios express the same departures in relative rather than absolute terms \parencite{taagepera1980proportionality}. At the party and coalition levels,
% \[
% R_i=\frac{s_i/S}{v_i/V},
% \qquad
% R_C=\frac{s_C/S}{v_C/V}
% =\frac{s_C}{q_C}
% =1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}
% =\sum_{i\in C}\frac{q_i}{q_C}R_i.
% \]

\[
R_i=\frac{s_i/S}{v_i/V}=\frac{s_i}{q_i},
\qquad
R_C=\frac{s_C/S}{v_C/V}
=\frac{s_C}{q_C}
=1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}
=\sum_{i\in C}\frac{q_i}{q_C}R_i.
\]
A value of one denotes exact proportionality, a value above one denotes
overrepresentation, and a value below one denotes underrepresentation. The ratios
permit comparisons across parties and coalitions of different sizes. The
differentials remain the more direct quantities for the majority-threshold
question because they measure the relevant departure in seats.


The national coalition differential can be decomposed by adding and subtracting
the seats that exact proportionality would assign to the coalition separately
within each district:
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_C
&=
\left[
\sum_d
\left(
s_{Cd}-S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
\right)
\right]
+
\left[
\left(
\sum_d S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
\right)
-
\left(
S\frac{v_C}{V}
\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
The first term compares the coalition's actual seats in each district with the
number implied by its vote share in that district. I denote it the
within-district component, \(A_C\). The other component is the between-district
component, \(B_C\). Its first term sums the coalition's proportional quota in
each district. The second is the quota implied by the coalition's share of the
national valid vote. These quantities need not coincide because districts differ
in the number of seats they contribute relative to the number of valid votes
cast in them. Thus, \(B_C\) depends on the geographic distribution of coalition
support across districts with different electoral weights. Support concentrated
in districts whose share of Chamber seats exceeds their share of the national
valid vote raises the sum of district quotas relative to the national quota,
while support concentrated in districts with the opposite weighting lowers it.

Thus, \(d_C=A_C+B_C\). For comparisons across coalitions of different electoral
sizes, the two components can also be expressed relative to the coalition's
proportional quota: \(R_C-1=\frac{A_C}{q_C}+\frac{B_C}{q_C}.\) This expresses
the within- and between-district contributions to the coalition's relative
departure from proportionality.

In Brazil, \(A_C\) can reflect the mechanisms that operate within each district
when votes are converted into seats: finite district magnitude, the
electoral-quotient and highest-averages calculations, the year-specific
eligibility rules for parties and candidates, and the pooling of votes through
proportional electoral coalitions in 2014 and 2018 and party federations in
2022. These mechanisms determine how the coalition's actual district seats
depart from its district-level proportional quotas. By contrast, \(B_C\) arises
from differences in electoral weight across districts and the geographic
distribution of coalition support: district apportionment, turnout, and
valid-vote variation determine how many seats correspond to a given number of
votes in each district, while coalition geography determines whether its support
is concentrated in relatively more or less heavily weighted districts. Neither
component isolates the independent effect of any one mechanism; the
decomposition just locates the coalition differential as a within or between
districts phenomenon \parencite{bochsler2026winners}.


The primary ideological universe is the set of parliamentary parties,
\(P^+=\{i\in P:s_i>0\}.\) National proportionality is evaluated over \(P\),
while potential parliamentary coalitions are constructed over \(P^+\). Thus,
votes for parties without seats remain part of \(V\) and of party-level
proportionality accounting, but those parties cannot constitute or interrupt a
primary ideological coalition. 

Axelrod's connected-coalition criterion provides the baseline restriction
\parencite{axelrod1970conflict}. Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) denote the existing election-year ideological order
restricted to \(P^+\), preserving the relative ordering of represented parties
without re-estimating their positions. A coalition \(C\subseteq P^+\) is connected when
its members form a contiguous interval in this filtered order. A connected
coalition is minimal winning when \(s_C\geq257\) and no proper connected subset
retains a Chamber majority. For an interval, removing either endpoint must
therefore leave fewer than 257 seats. Exact connectedness (\(k=0\)) provides the
baseline ideological domain. As a sensitivity analysis, I use a one-gap domain (\(k=1\)), which
allows at most one represented party inside a coalition's ideological
span to be omitted. A winning coalition is minimal within this domain
if no proper subset satisfying the same restriction retains a majority.\footnote{
Appendix~\ref{app:one-gap} gives the formal definition and summary results.
I also repeat both searches using the full election-year party order,
including parties that received votes but no seats, so that
non-seat-winning parties can interrupt connectedness. Connectedness and
minimality are recomputed in this universe, while the underlying vote-seat
data and national vote denominator remain unchanged.
Appendix~\ref{app:ideological-universes} compares the results.
Full coalition-level results are available in the replication outputs.}


\subsection{Data and operationalization}
\label{sec:data}

Vote and seat data for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 federal-deputy elections
come from the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral, accessed through the
\texttt{electionsBR} package \parencite{electionsbr}.\footnote{All 2022
results refer to the official seat vector in the frozen dataset, prior
to subsequent judicial retotalizations.} For each party, the national
vote total combines valid candidate votes and party-label votes across
the twenty-seven districts, while the seat total counts deputies elected
under its election-year label.\footnote{For 2014, the vote measure is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS + QT\_VOTOS\_LEGENDA}. For 2018 and 2022,
it is \texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS\_VALIDOS +
QT\_TOTAL\_VOTOS\_LEG\_VALIDOS}.} This attribution also applies to
parties that competed through proportional electoral coalitions in
2014 and 2018 or federations in 2022. The resulting party totals connect
the district electoral outcomes to the party identities observed in
cabinet participation and ideological classifications.

Cabinet composition is reconstructed from publicly available online records for
the Dilma Rousseff, Michel Temer, Jair Bolsonaro, and Lula III administrations,
recording each officeholder's service dates and party affiliation at the time. I
map these dated affiliations to the party labels of the election that produced
the corresponding legislative term and treat each distinct party set within that
term as one cabinet configuration\footnote{For compact reference, cabinet
configurations are labeled \textit{YY-nn}, where \textit{YY} identifies the
election whose vote--seat distribution is used and \textit{nn} gives the
configuration's order of first appearance in the corresponding legislative-term
cabinet chronology. Thus, 14-05 denotes the fifth distinct configuration
associated with the 2014 election, 18-11 the eleventh associated with 2018, and
22-01 the first associated with 2022. These labels are identifiers, not dates;
a recurring configuration retains the same label.}.

Each cabinet configuration is evaluated against the vote and seat distribution
of the election that produced the corresponding Chamber: 2014 for the
2015--2018 term, 2018 for 2019--2022, and 2022 for Lula III through March
19, 2026. Parties that renamed or merged during a term are matched to the
election-year parties from which their vote and seat totals derive\footnote{
Brazilian parties are generally identified by their election-year party
abbreviations rather than by their full names.}. This yields
12 distinct cabinet configurations for the 2014 term, 18 for 2018, and 5 for
2022\footnote{Appendix~\ref{app:composition} reports their membership.}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:party}

Before turning to coalitions, I first examine the party-level seat differentials
\(d_i\) and representation ratios \(R_i\) produced in each election, which
describe the absolute and relative departures from national proportionality
that are subsequently combined at the coalition level.


In the 2014 election PMDB received 8.13 seats above its national proportional
quota, the largest positive differential that year. PSD and PTB received another
4.55 and 4.37 seats above their quotas, respectively. These gains coexisted with
deficits of 4.43 seats for PSDB and 4.20 for PSOL. The connected inversions
identified below all combine PMDB and PSD with PSDB: their majority advantage
therefore does not require every member to be overrepresented.

and on whether their total bridges the distance to 257 seats.

The positive within-district component of the two inversions extends to all 35
cabinet configurations: \(A_C>0\) in every case, while \(B_C>0\) in only
thirteen. Cabinet composition helps explain this regularity. In every
configuration, the mean national vote share of cabinet-member parties exceeds
that of nonmember parties in the same election-year party system, with the
difference ranging from 1.46 to 4.95 percentage points. Larger parties are also
usually on the positive side of the within-district component: among parties
receiving at least 5 percent of the national vote, \(A_i>0\) for six of seven in
2014, eight of nine in 2018, and all seven in 2022\footnote{
Here \(A_i\) denotes the within-district component for party \(i\).
Appendix~\ref{app:party-contributions} gives the party-to-coalition
identities and selected member-party contributions.}. In 34 of the 35 cabinet
configurations, the positive \(A_i\) contributions of members above this
benchmark exceed the negative contributions of all members combined; only one
configuration also requires positive contributions from smaller parties to keep
\(A_C>0\). Cabinet composition therefore repeatedly brings together relatively
large parties whose within-district contributions are predominantly positive.
No comparable sign pattern appears for \(B_C\).

The two inverted cabinet party sets differ in how the positive \(A_C\)
interacts with between-district weighting. For 14-05, \(A_C=15.74\)
raises the coalition's quota of 238.73 to \(q_C+A_C=254.47\) seats,
still short of a Chamber majority. Adding \(B_C=4.53\) brings the
observed total to 259 seats. For 22-01, by contrast, \(A_C=13.20\)
already exceeds the 6.20 seats separating the quota from a majority:
\(q_C+A_C=263.99\). The negative between-district component,
\(B_C=-0.99\), reduces the total to 263 seats without removing the
majority. Thus, within-district gains characterize both inversions, while
between-district weighting reinforces the Dilma coalition and offsets the
Lula coalition. Only in the former is the positive between-district
component also needed to bridge the proportional distance to 257 seats.

Figure~\ref{fig:minimal-connected-inversions} reveals three different
coalitional structures behind the same formal outcome. In 2014, inversion is
available through several overlapping combinations to PT's right. In 2018, it
appears only in one broad interval extending from PT through PSDB. In 2022,
it again occurs to PT's right, but now includes the compact seven-party
PP--PL interval. Coalition inversion is therefore not attached to a fixed
ideological location or coalition size. What changes across elections is which
regions of the party order contain combinations whose accumulated
representational advantages are sufficient to turn a vote minority into a
seat majority.


Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} shows whether these minimal cases remain inverted as
their connected intervals expand. Blue cells have both vote and seat majorities,
red cells are minimal connected inversions, and orange cells are larger
connected coalitions that remain inverted. Whereas Figure~\ref{fig:minimal-connected-inversions} identifies the
membership and endpoints of the minimal cases, the heatmaps show how those cases
sit within the surrounding space of connected coalitions.

The structure differs substantially across elections. In 2014, several
minimal inversions lie in overlapping center-to-right spans, and some are
accompanied by orange cells. The inversion is therefore not confined to the
minimal winning coalitions themselves: some larger connected intervals
also retain a seat majority while remaining below 50 percent of the national
vote. In 2018, the blue region of vote-and-seat majorities contracts and its
boundary shifts toward intervals extending farther to the right of the party
ordering. At the same time, PT--PSDB is the only inversion and there are
no orange cells. This is a distinct feature of the 2018 configuration. The
PT--PSDB interval has 49.95 percent of the vote and 267 seats, but any
larger exact-connected interval containing it also has a vote majority. The
single inversion is therefore isolated within the surrounding connected
coalition space rather than forming part of a larger set of inverted intervals.

The 2022 pattern differs again. The blue region is smaller, reflecting a lower
proportion of connected coalitions with both vote and seat majorities, while the
inversion cells are concentrated on the right. Unlike 2018, orange cells
reappear: some connected expansions of the minimal right-side inversions
continue to hold a Chamber majority without reaching 50 percent of the national
vote. Connected majorities extending through PT, by contrast, require broader
intervals and have both vote and seat majorities. The 2022 result is therefore
not merely the existence of two minimal inversions, but an asymmetry in where
connected vote-and-seat majorities and connected vote-minority seat majorities
occur within the party ordering.

Figure~\ref{fig:minimal-connected-inversions} and
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} thus reveal different aspects of the same result:
the first identifies the coalitions at the minimal winning boundary, while
the second shows whether inversion remains present as the connected
interval is expanded. The right-side concentration in 2022 is consistent
with the party-level results above, where large right and center-right parties,
particularly PL and UNIÃO, carry substantial positive seat differentials
that can reinforce one another when adjacent parties are combined.

quota: the horizontal coordinate is \(100A_C/q_C\), and the vertical coordinate
is \(100B_C/q_C\). Since \(d_C=A_C+B_C\) and
\(R_C-1=d_C/q_C\), their sum is \(100(R_C-1)\). Coalitions with the
same representation ratio therefore lie on the same line with slope \(-1\),
while different values of \(R_C\) define parallel lines. The dashed diagonal
marks \(R_C=1\), or exact proportionality. 

All cabinet sets in Panel A lie to the right of \(A_C=0\), so their members
collectively benefit from the within-district translation of votes into seats.
What distinguishes them is how that support is distributed across districts with
different electoral weights. The 2014 election provides the clearest contrast.
All six Dilma configurations have \(B_C>0\): their member parties, taken
together, draw relatively more support from districts contributing more Chamber
seats per valid vote. All six Temer configurations have \(B_C<0\), indicating the opposite pattern of between-district weighting.. Because both sequences are evaluated against
the same 2014 electoral distribution, the sign reversal comes from cabinet
recomposition rather than from a new election. The transition from Dilma to
Temer therefore changes not only which parties compose the government, but also
how the government's electoral support is positioned within the territorial
weighting of the Chamber.

The Bolsonaro cabinet sets extend farther into the negative between-district
region. This reflects a recurrent territorial disadvantage in the geographic
distribution of their electoral support. PSL appears in fourteen of the eighteen
configurations.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{app:composition} lists the election-year
  member parties of each cabinet configuration.} All fourteen have \(B_C<0\),
and PSL contributes \(-5.28\) seats to the between-district total. NOVO, PRB,
PSDB, and PR also contribute negatively, whereas PP, MDB, PSC, PSD, and DEM
contribute positively. Taken together, Bolsonaro's cabinet parties therefore
tended to draw relatively more support from districts contributing fewer Chamber
seats per valid vote and relatively less support from districts with greater
electoral weight. The persistence of \(B_C<0\) across changing cabinet
configurations suggests that this was not specific to a single party set, but a
recurring feature of how the cabinet parties' combined support was distributed
across districts.

Unlike cabinet sets, minimal connected winning coalitions appear on both sides
of \(A_C=0\), reflecting differences in whether their members collectively gain
or lose seats in the within-district translation of votes into seats. Their
between-district contributions also vary. The 2018 PT--PSDB inversion, for
example, combines \(A_C=6.57\) with \(B_C=4.18\). Its members therefore benefit
both from the within-district allocation of seats and from the geographic
distribution of their support: taken together, they perform relatively better
in districts contributing more Chamber seats per valid vote. Most Bolsonaro
cabinet sets exhibit a different territorial profile. Their positive
within-district gains are partly offset because their members' combined support
is relatively concentrated in districts with lower electoral weight. The same
2018 electoral distribution can therefore advantage different party
combinations through different channels, depending on which parties are brought
together.

A broader difference between the two coalition domains is visible in their
distance from the \(R_C=1\) diagonal. The minimal connected winning coalitions
are generally much closer to exact proportionality than the cabinet party sets:
seventeen of the twenty lie between \(R_C=0.95\) and \(R_C=1.05\), compared with
fourteen of the thirty-five cabinet sets \footnote{
For the ideological coalitions, Appendix~\ref{app:minimal-connected-winning}
reports the vote shares and seat totals from which these ratios
can be recovered.}. Their combined seat shares therefore
tend to track their combined vote shares more closely than those of cabinet
party sets. Yet seven of these coalitions still invert. Coalition inversion
consequently need not arise from a large aggregate departure from
proportionality. For coalitions already close to the majority threshold, a
comparatively small net representational advantage can be sufficient to reverse
vote and seat majority status.

The 2022 cases also show the limit of this pattern. MDB--UNI\~AO remains close
to the proportionality diagonal, with \(R_C=1.050\), but its modest advantage
is enough to produce an inversion because its 49.22 percent vote share already
places it close to a majority. PP--PL is the conspicuous exception in Panel B:
with \(R_C=1.111\), it is the minimal connected coalition farthest from exact
proportionality. Its much larger representational advantage allows a coalition
with only 45.26 percent of the vote to reach 258 seats. The ideological
inversions thus include both modest departures that become consequential near
the majority threshold and a more pronounced case in which overrepresentation
compensates for a substantially larger vote deficit.


\section{Discussion}


The first result is a contrast between realized rarity and a broader range of
potential inversions. Among the 35 distinct cabinet party sets, only two invert.
Among the twenty minimal connected winning coalitions, seven do. Cabinet
participation therefore shows that coalition inversion was institutionally
realized, but only in a small number of the governing party configurations
actually observed. The ideological analysis shows, in turn, that these cabinets
do not exhaust the politically structured combinations under which the same
electoral distributions can reverse vote and seat majorities. The relaxation of
exact connectedness reinforces this interpretation. Allowing one represented
party inside an ideological span to be omitted preserves the strongest
inversions in 2014 and 2022 and reveals a stronger case in 2018, where a
coalition with 47.59 percent of the vote reaches exactly 257 seats\footnote{
Appendix~\ref{app:one-gap} compares the strongest inversions
under exact connectedness and the one-gap relaxation.}.


A second implication is that inversion is not simply a stronger form of
overrepresentation. Overrepresentation is common among cabinet coalitions: 33 of
the 35 sets have \(R_C>1\), yet only two combine a vote minority with a seat
majority. \(R_C\) measures the coalition's advantage relative to its vote share,
while \(d_C\) expresses that advantage in seats. Whether this advantage changes
majority status also depends on \(q_C\), which determines how far the coalition
lies from 257 seats under exact proportionality. A positive differential is
therefore insufficient for inversion unless it is large enough to bridge that
remaining gap to a seat majority. 



The Brazilian case adds a second stage to the aggregation problem identified
by Kurrild-Klitgaard and Miller. At the coalition level, positive and negative
party differentials reinforce or offset one another as parties are combined.
In Brazil, however, those party differentials are themselves national
aggregates of twenty-seven independently determined district outcomes. The
decomposition distinguishes the part of the coalition differential associated
with departures from proportionality within districts from the part arising
because the sum of district-level proportional quotas differs from the
coalition's national quota.  Both components carry into national party seat totals and, ultimately, into
coalition majority status.

Cabinet recomposition changes not only the parties represented in government but
also the distribution of their combined support across districts with different
electoral weights. The Dilma and Temer configurations draw on the same 2014
election, yet the former benefit from between-district weighting while the
latter are disadvantaged by it. Its contribution therefore depends on which
parties are combined rather than providing governing coalitions with a uniform
advantage.


The decomposition also places the inversion cases within a broader set of
coalition configurations. Some of the patterns observed
among inverted coalitions are not distinctive to inversion itself. All 35
distinct cabinet party sets have a positive within-district component,
whereas the between-district component is positive in only thirteen. The
two cabinet inversions therefore belong to a much wider pattern in which
cabinet composition repeatedly combines positive within-district
contributions, including in party sets that remain minorities in both votes
and seats or majorities in both. The party-level contributions help explain
this regularity: larger cabinet members often supply substantial positive
\(A_i\) contributions, while smaller parties can reinforce or offset them.


The ideological coalitions also show that similar majority outcomes can rest on
different representational advantages. MDB--UNIÃO benefits chiefly from the
territorial distribution of its support, whereas PP--PL obtains most of its
surplus through vote-to-seat translation within districts. The one-gap results
extend this contrast: favorable territorial weighting often provides the larger
contribution among the 2022 inversions, even though positive within-district
gains remain widespread. Component size must also be distinguished from its role
in reaching the majority threshold. A relatively small positive \(B_C\) can
provide the remaining seats needed after \(A_C\) has brought a coalition close
to 257, even when \(A_C\) accounts for most of the total differential. The
decomposition thus shows that coalitions with the same inversion status, and
even coalitions with similar overall representational advantages, can occupy
quite different positions with respect to within-district allocation and
between-district weighting.

This result deepens Miller's aggregation argument in an important respect. A
fixed electoral distribution does not imply a fixed political beneficiary of
coalition inversion. The same party vote and seat totals can sustain different
majority relations across governing configurations and can place potential
inversions in different regions of the party system. The Dilma inversion ends
when the remaining cabinet parties lose their seat majority, whereas the initial
Lula inversion ends when the expanded cabinet acquires a vote majority as well.
Neither transition reflects a new electoral allocation. The same party vote and
seat totals instead support different coalition-level majority relations at
different moments of the legislative term.

The ideological results show that this variation is not only temporal. In 2022,
the electoral allocation supports both Lula's broad, sparse, left-anchored
cabinet inversion and potential connected inversions on the right, including the
compact PP--PL interval\footnote{
  Figure~\ref{fig:cabinet-membership-inversions} in
  Appendix~\ref{app:cabinet-interval-bridge} displays the cabinet's membership
  in the election-year ideological order.}. These are politically different but
overlapping combinations: UNIÃO belongs to both the cabinet and PP--PL, carrying
its positive seat differential into each. The same party-level advantage can
therefore contribute to majorities with different political anchors. The 2018
election provides the converse pattern: no observed cabinet party set inverts,
even though an exact-connected inversion exists and the one-gap relaxation
reveals a stronger one. Party-level overrepresentation consequently does not
imply a stable advantage for one governing bloc or ideological side. Large
parties can contribute substantial positive or negative differentials, but which
political combinations benefit from them depends on how those contributions are
distributed across coalitions. The analysis identifies these representational
consequences of coalition structure; it does not imply that parties select
partners in order to exploit them.

Notice that cabinet membership identifies
the parties represented in government, not the coalition supporting the
executive in every legislative vote. Likewise, the vote share attributed to
a cabinet or potential coalition is obtained by summing the election-year
votes of its member parties; it is not a direct electoral endorsement of the
later coalition as a collective actor. The ideological analysis imposes a
different restriction: admissible coalitions are defined relative to a fixed
estimated election-year party order, so alternative party placements can
change the boundaries of connected or one-gap coalitions. Finally, the
\(A_C+B_C\) decomposition separates the observed coalition differential
into a within-district allocation component and a between-district weighting
component. It does not identify the independent causal effects of district
magnitude, thresholds, electoral pooling, malapportionment, turnout, or the
other institutional and geographical processes that contribute to those
components.





\section{Conclusion}

The Brazilian case shows that coalition inversion is neither merely a
theoretical possibility nor a routine feature of coalition politics under
proportional representation. It occurs among observed governing party sets, but
rarely: only two of the 35 distinct cabinet configurations invert. The same
electoral distributions, however, permit a broader range of inversions among
ideologically structured parliamentary majorities. Seven of the twenty minimal
connected winning coalitions invert, with cases in all three elections. The
one-gap analysis reveals further inversions beyond the exact-connected cases.
The central empirical result is therefore a contrast between realized rarity and
a wider set of politically structured inversion possibilities.

The institutional consequence is not simply that some parties are
overrepresented. Coalition composition determines which representational
advantages are combined into a majority and which are offset by partners'
disadvantages. The cabinet results show that governing combinations can
be advantaged or disadvantaged by between-district weighting
under the same electoral allocation.
The ideological results show that this allocation can make compact
majorities available in one part of the party order while requiring
broader combinations elsewhere. Coalition inversion identifies when these
differences in coalition composition become a reversal between vote and
seat majorities.

Proportional representation is usually described through the relationship
between each party's votes and seats. Once those allocations are combined
into parliamentary majorities, however, an additional structure becomes
visible. Coalition inversion identifies the configurations in which ordinary
party-level deviations are sufficient to reverse the relation between vote
majorities and seat majorities. This structure makes it possible to ask where
such reversals lie within a party system, whether they are realized in
governing coalitions, and how their incidence changes as the same legislature
is recombined. At the same time, the relation being reversed is a basic one
for democratic representation: a set of parties supported by less than half
of the national vote can control a majority of legislative seats. Coalition
inversions therefore reveal both an analytical property of proportional
systems and a majoritarian tension within the parliamentary outcomes those
systems can produce.

\subsection{Admissible domains and the one-gap relaxation}

Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) order the parties in \(P^*\), where \(P^*=P^+\)
contains seat-winning parties in the primary specification and \(P^*=P\)
contains all parties in the sensitivity analysis. For nonempty
\(C\subseteq P^*\), let \(a=\min\{j:p_j\in C\}\) and
\(b=\max\{j:p_j\in C\}\). Define
\[
\operatorname{span}(C)=\{p_a,\ldots,p_b\},\qquad
 g(C)=|\operatorname{span}(C)\setminus C|,\qquad
 \mathcal D_k=\{\varnothing\ne C\subseteq P^*:g(C)\leq k\}.
\]
Thus, \(k=0\) requires connectedness, while \(k=1\) permits at most one
omitted party inside the span. A winning \(C\) is minimal only if no proper
subset admissible in the same \(\mathcal D_k\) retains at least 257 seats.
Minimality is recomputed for each \(k\) and party universe: nested admissible
domains do not imply nested collections of minimal winning coalitions.

compares the counts. The  one-gap domain contains 100 inversions among

\subsection{Sensitivity to the ideological party universe}
\label{app:ideological-universes}

Restoring parties without seats removes the 2018 exact-connected PT--PSDB
inversion: its interval includes PMB and acquires a vote majority. The strongest
2014 and 2022 endpoint regions persist. Under all-party \(k=0\), the strongest
cases remain PTB--PR in 2014, with 47.59 percent of the vote and 257 seats, and
PP--PL in 2022, with 45.35 percent and 258 seats. Among the six all-party
\(k=0\) inversions, \(A_C>0\) in five; MDB--UNIÃO is the sole exception.
Under all-party \(k=1\), their strongest
cases omit PPL and DC, respectively, recovering the  PTB--PR and PP--PL
memberships and outcomes. The strongest 2018 all-party one-gap case is
PCdoB--PODE omitting PSDB, with 47.82 percent of the vote and the same 257 seats,
compared with 47.59 percent in the seat-winning one-gap specification.
