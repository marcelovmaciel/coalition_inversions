\begin{abstract}
  Proportional representation is usually evaluated party by party, by comparing
each party's share of seats with its share of votes. Yet fragmented
legislatures are governed through combinations of parties, so small
party-level deviations can become consequential when parties are combined.
Brazil provides a useful setting because the Chamber of Deputies is assembled
from twenty-seven separate district elections. Several institutional steps
separate national votes from final party seat totals, and no national
compensatory allocation reconciles them. I ask whether politically meaningful
sets of parties can hold a legislative majority despite receiving less than
half of all valid federal-deputy votes nationwide, a condition known as
coalition inversion. Using the 2014, 2018, and 2022 elections, I compare distinct
election-year cabinet party sets with potential coalitions restricted by
ideological proximity. Cabinet inversions appear
following the 2014 and 2022 elections, but not the 2018 election; inversions
among minimal connected winning coalitions appear in all three. In 2022, a seven-party bloc of ideologically adjacent
parties combined 45.26 percent of
the vote with 258 of 513 seats. Party-level deviations from proportionality can therefore
have a distinct coalition-level consequence: they can determine which
combinations of parties command a legislative majority.

\section{Introduction}

Under proportional representation, vote--seat translation is usually assessed
party by party, by comparing each party's vote and seat shares
\parencite{kaminski26_propor_repres_primer}. In fragmented legislatures,
however, governing and legislative majorities are assembled from several
parties. Combining parties can make individually modest deviations
consequential. Positive and negative deviations may cancel or reinforce one
another, allowing a party set to cross the legislative majority threshold before
its combined vote share reaches one half. This configuration is a coalition
inversion: a party set holds a seat majority while receiving less than half of
all valid national legislative votes.
 


\textcite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres} documents empirical cases of coalition inversions in Danish government
formation. \textcite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres} establishes the more general result that inversions remain
possible even when seats are allocated in a single national constituency,
without an explicit seat threshold, and by a highly proportional formula.
Because seats must be assigned in whole numbers, party seat shares will
generally depart slightly from exact proportionality. Once positive and negative
party-level deviations exist, some combinations of parties can reverse the vote
and seat majorities. These results settle the existence question. They do not
show how coalition inversions appear when national party seat totals are
themselves assembled from many district elections.

Brazil provides such a setting. The Chamber of Deputies is not formed by
applying one proportional allocation to national party votes. Its seats are
allocated separately across the twenty-six states and the Federal District.
District magnitudes and electoral weights vary, electoral rules and the units
over which votes were pooled changed across the three elections, and party
support is distributed unevenly across the country. Several institutional and
geographical mediations therefore stand between a party's national vote share
and its final national seat share \parencite{bochsler2026winners}. 

This institutional structure changes the sources of deviation without changing
the national proportionality benchmark. I compare seat shares with shares of all
valid federal-deputy votes, including votes for parties that obtain no seats.
Restricting the denominator to votes for seat-winning parties would instead
evaluate representation conditional on which parties entered the Chamber. The
question here concerns the national outcome of the entire vote-to-seat process,
including electoral exclusion. Such exclusion can affect the distribution
of seats among represented parties, as work on spoiler effects under
proportional representation demonstrates
\parencite{kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system}. The benchmark therefore
measures departures from national proportionality without presuming that those
departures are unintended or unjustified.

Party-level deviations emerge from twenty-seven district-level
translations operating under heterogeneous conditions, rather than only
from rounding a single national allocation. The organizing question is
therefore: when do these deviations combine so that a politically
meaningful set of parties holds a legislative majority without a national
vote majority? The contribution lies in showing how the resulting
party-level seat differentials aggregate across coalitions and change
majority status.

The Chamber contains many mathematically possible party subsets, most with
little political meaning. The first empirical domain therefore uses realized
cabinet coalitions. Changes in ministerial participation generate successive
party sets during a legislative term;
for each, I compare the Chamber seats of its members with their votes in the
corresponding election.

The second domain considers potential coalitions restricted by ideological
proximity. The baseline is the set of minimal connected winning coalitions:
contiguous blocs in the election-year left--right order that hold a seat majority
and lose it if narrowed \parencite{axelrod1970conflict}. A one-gap 
check permits one represented party inside the span to be omitted. For both
domains, I analyze the coalition seat differential and its  decomposition
into within-district and between-district components.

Inversions appear in both coalition domains, but their distribution differs
across elections. Two of the 35 distinct cabinet party sets invert: one observed for five days
under Dilma Rousseff following the 2014 election and one observed during the first 255
days of Lula III following the 2022 election. No cabinet party set linked to
the 2018 election inverts in the primary chronology. Minimal connected
winning inversions, by contrast, appear in all three elections. The 2022
cases include a compact right-side bloc with 45.26 percent of the vote and
258 seats. Within-district and between-district components reinforce one
another in some inversions and offset one another in others.

\section{Brazil's multidistrict open-list proportional system}
\label{sec:brazil-system}

Coalition majorities are formed from national party seat totals, but those
totals are not produced by a national allocation. In Brazil, elections to the
Chamber of Deputies take place separately in the twenty-six states and the
Federal District, each of which forms an electoral district. The 513 seats are
allocated within these twenty-seven districts and then summed nationally. The
Federal District is simply an eight-seat district, and no compensatory tier
subsequently adjusts the resulting party totals, unlike compensatory mixed
systems, where a second allocation stage can correct constituency-level
disproportionality \parencite{FlisGrofmanKaminski2025Introduction, salamon2026mixed}.

District magnitude varies considerably across states, from eight to seventy
seats. Eleven districts elect only eight deputies, the median magnitude is ten,
the mean nineteen, and S\~ao Paulo elects seventy. These differences imply
substantial variation in the effective conditions of representation across
districts: winning a seat is generally more demanding where fewer seats are
available. Because each district is allocated independently, the resulting
advantages and shortfalls carry into national party seat totals.

Within each district, voters encounter an open-list election. They may enter the identifying number of an individual candidate or cast a vote for a party label. A candidate vote performs two functions at once. It contributes to the aggregate vote of the party, electoral coalition, or federation through which the candidate competes, and it raises that candidate's position within the relevant open list. A party-label vote contributes to the first calculation but not to the ranking among candidates. That is, votes first determine how many seats an electoral list receives. Nominal votes then determine which candidates within that list take the seats, subject to the individual eligibility requirements in force for the election. A candidate can therefore benefit from votes cast for copartisans or electoral partners, but cannot ordinarily occupy a seat unless the list to which the candidate belongs wins one \parencite{nicolau2006openlist}.

Valid candidate and party-label votes are first aggregated for the electoral
unit in force in district $d$, whether a party, proportional coalition, or
federation. The electoral quotient and party quotient are
\(QE_d=\operatorname{round}_{\mathrm{stat}}\!\left(\frac{V_d}{M_d}\right)\) and
\(QP_{\ell d}=\left\lfloor\frac{v_{\ell d}}{QE_d}\right\rfloor .\) The party
quotient determines the initial list seats, subject to the candidate-eligibility
rules applicable in that election
\parencite[arts.~106--108]{codigoEleitoral1965}.

Unallocated seats are then assigned sequentially by highest averages,
$v_{\ell d}/(s_{\ell d}+1)$, producing the Jefferson--D'Hondt sequence
$v_{\ell d}/1,v_{\ell d}/2,\ldots$ until all seats are filled. Eligibility for
these remainder seats follows the year-specific rules described below
\parencite[art.~109]{codigoEleitoral1965,nicolauSchmitt1995}. 

Eligibility rules changed across elections. In 2014, there was no fixed
candidate threshold, but only lists reaching the electoral quotient could contest
remainders \parencite[art.~186, \S 2]{tse2013res23399}. In 2018, candidates
ordinarily needed 10 percent of the quotient; all lists could contest remainders
while an eligible candidate remained, after which any still-unfilled seats were
assigned by highest averages without that nominal-vote minimum
\parencite[arts.~7--10]{tse2017res23554}. In 2022, the 10 percent candidate
threshold remained for initial seats. For remainder seats, lists first needed
80 percent of the quotient and candidates 20 percent; any seats still unfilled
were then assigned by highest averages among lists meeting the 80 percent
requirement, without the 20 percent candidate threshold
\parencite[arts.~8--11]{tse2021res23677}.

Moreover, proportional electoral coalitions, or \emph{coligações proporcionais}, were
part of the 2014 and 2018 elections. They were formed within the electoral
circumscription, which for the Chamber meant a state or the Federal District. A
party could consequently enter different proportional arrangements in different
states, subject to the legal restrictions in force for the election. These were
not national alliances whose composition automatically carried across all
twenty-seven contests \parencite{nicolau2006openlist,tse2013res23399,tse2017res23554}. Member parties of proportional electoral coalitions legally operated as a
joint open list when votes were converted into seats.
Candidate votes and party-label votes credited to any member entered the
coalition's district total; the quotient and highest-averages calculations then
assigned seats to the coalition as a whole. Those seats were occupied by the
highest nominal vote-getters across the combined list, regardless of the member
party to which they belonged. A candidate from one party could therefore be
elected partly because votes cast for candidates or labels of another coalition
member raised the joint total \parencite{nicolau2006openlist,tse2013res23399,tse2017res23554}. 

This resembles list apparentement because parties pool votes before seats are
settled, but the Brazilian arrangement differs from the two-stage system studied
by \textcite{pukelsheimLeutgaeb2013apparentements}. There, alliance seats are
subsequently apportioned among component lists. In Brazil, a single joint open
list selected winners by candidate votes, so gains to particular members depended
on the distribution of nominal votes rather than on a fixed secondary
apportionment rule.

Constitutional reform abolished proportional electoral coalitions, and party
federations were subsequently introduced as a new form of multiparty
organization for proportional elections. Three federations competed in
2022: PT--PCdoB--PV, PSDB--Cidadania, and PSOL--REDE
\parencite{tse2026registeredFederations}. Like the former
\emph{coliga\c{c}\~oes}, federations pooled their members' votes in a common
open list, so constituent-party seat totals resulted from competition within
the pooled list rather than from independent proportional allocations.

Unlike the former electoral coalitions, federations operate nationally and in
principle persist for at least four years. They have some common congressional
procedures, including leadership and committee-allocation arrangements, but do
not merge their constituent parties, which retain separate identities and
organizational autonomy \parencite{tse2021res23670}.


National proportionality provides a common baseline against which to assess how
Brazil’s layered vote-to-seat process generates representational deviations and
when those deviations become majority-changing as parties are recombined into
coalitions. If \(v_i\) is the national valid vote total credited to party \(i\)
and \(V\) is the corresponding total for all parties, including those that win
no seats, the party's reference quota is \(513v_i/V\) seats. Brazil's actual
system never performs this national allocation.

Several mediations therefore stand between a party's national vote share and its national seat share. Within each district, finite magnitude, the quotient and highest-averages calculations, legal eligibility thresholds, and the number and relative strength of competing electoral units shape the conversion of votes into seats. Electoral pooling adds a further mediation by changing both the vote total on which seats are allocated and, through the common candidate list, the eventual distribution of those seats among member parties. These arrangements varied across districts in 2014 and 2018 and became nationally fixed through federations in 2022.

The national seat distribution then aggregates twenty-seven independently determined district outcomes. Chamber seats are apportioned by population, but the constitutional floor of eight and ceiling of seventy prevent exact population-to-seat equality across states \parencite{samuelsSnyder2001value,brasil1993lc78}. Turnout and valid-vote rates also differ across elections and districts, so a seat can correspond to different numbers of valid votes in different states. Because parties distribute their support unevenly across the country, national vote shares can therefore be assembled from geographically different configurations with different representational consequences. Electoral geography interacts with district seat weights and local competitive conditions before the resulting party seats are summed nationally. The resulting disproportionality need not have a single source: allocation rules, district magnitude, malapportionment and electoral weight, electoral geography, and party competition constitute distinct channels through which party vote and seat shares can diverge \parencite{bochsler2026winners}.

This layered translation occurs in an unusually fragmented party environment. Measured by the Laakso--Taagepera index, the effective numbers of electoral parties were 14.11 in 2014, 18.02 in 2018, and 12.34 in 2022; the corresponding effective numbers of parliamentary parties were 13.33, 16.41, and 9.91 \parencite{laakso1979effective}. The exact number of allocation units was not identical to the number of party labels, especially when district coalitions or national federations pooled votes. Even so, each election produced a Chamber in which legislative majorities had to be assembled across numerous partisan units. Fragmentation makes the political consequences of modest party-level gains and losses depend on how those parties are combined.

The national party seat totals produced by this process enter a presidential
system in which governing capacity ordinarily depends on multiparty
arrangements. Brazilian coalitional presidentialism links the presidency to
combinations of parties across cabinet and Congress: ministries are distributed
among several parties, while executive--legislative coordination is organized
through agreements among them
\parencite{abranches1988presidencialismo,figueiredo2000presidential}.
The Chamber assembled from twenty-seven district elections therefore supplies
the parliamentary resources from which governing coalitions are constructed.
Party-level representation is politically consequential not only in isolation,
but also through the combinations of parties that can command a legislative
majority.

 The realized party seat totals incorporate the effects of rounding,
highest averages, district magnitude, eligibility requirements, electoral
pooling, apportionment, turnout, valid-vote variation, electoral geography, and
district-level competition. Comparing those totals with national proportional
quotas identifies the positive and negative representational deviations carried
by each party.

\section{Coalition inversions, domains, and measurement}
\label{sec:mea}



Party seat totals need not equal their national proportional quotas. When parties
are combined, their positive and negative differentials may cancel or reinforce.
A coalition inversion occurs when the accumulated differential carries a party
set above the Chamber's 257-seat threshold before its combined vote reaches one
half of the national total. The possibility of coalition inversions under proportional representation is already established by Kurrild-Klitgaard \parencite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres} and Miller \parencite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres}. The present analysis extends this agenda to a distinct institutional setting, where national party seat totals emerge from twenty-seven independent district allocations and several additional electoral mediations, and to coalition domains defined by realized cabinet participation and ideological proximity.

Because a fragmented legislature permits many mathematically possible party
sets, I restrict the coalition space in two ways. Observed cabinets identify
realized governing combinations; ideological connectedness defines a structured
domain of potential coalitions. The two domains show, respectively, how
party-level differentials combine in government and where potential inversions
appear in the ideological ordering.

Cabinet coalitions are substantively meaningful because Brazilian presidents ordinarily govern through multiparty arrangements. Cabinet participation records a party combination that was not merely arithmetically possible but institutionally realized in executive government. The relevant representational question is, thus, whether the members of the party set holding ministries collectively possessed a Chamber majority despite receiving less than half of the valid federal-deputy vote in the election that produced that legislature.

Cabinet membership changes during a legislative term while the election-year
vote and seat vector remains fixed. These transitions recombine the same
party-level differentials, allowing inversion status to change without a new
electoral allocation. The cabinet sequence therefore shows whether, and for how
long, realized governing sets cross the seat-majority threshold without a vote
majority.

Observed cabinets cover only a small share of possible parliamentary majorities,
so I also examine potential coalitions structured by ideological proximity.
Following \textcite{axelrod1970conflict}, I use minimal connected winning
coalitions: contiguous intervals in the election-year ideological order that
hold at least 257 seats and lose that majority when narrowed. This domain
identifies where ideologically contiguous party sets first acquire a seat
majority. The inversion question can then be asked directly at that frontier:
which of these ideologically coherent seat majorities still fall short of a
national vote majority?


\subsection{Measurement }

Let \(P\) denote the set of all parties receiving valid votes in a
federal-deputy election, including those that win no seats. For each party \(i\in P\),
let \(v_i\) be its national total of valid candidate and party-label
votes and \(s_i\) its Chamber seats. With \(V=\sum_{i\in P}v_i\) denoting
the total valid federal-deputy vote and \(S=513\), exact national
proportionality assigns party \(i\) the quota
\[
q_i=S\frac{v_i}{V}.
\]

The party seat differential is $d_i=s_i-q_i$; positive values denote
more seats than the national quota and negative values fewer. Because seats and
quotas each sum to $S$, party differentials sum to zero. For coalition
$C\subseteq P$,
\[
v_C=\sum_{i\in C}v_i,\quad s_C=\sum_{i\in C}s_i,\quad
q_C=S\frac{v_C}{V},\quad d_C=s_C-q_C=\sum_{i\in C}d_i.
\]

The coalition's departure from exact national proportionality is produced by
adding the positive and negative differentials of its member parties. A party
with a negative contribution can belong to an inverted coalition, provided that
the positive contributions elsewhere in the set more than offset it.

A coalition inversion occurs when the party set receives less than half of the
national valid federal-deputy vote but controls at least 257 Chamber seats:
\(\frac{v_C}{V}<\frac{1}{2}\) and \(s_C\geq257.\)
For a vote-minority coalition, the seat-majority condition is
$d_C\geq257-q_C$: its accumulated differential must bridge the distance from
its national proportional quota to 257 seats. Because $C$ and its complement
partition both votes and seats, an inversion simultaneously gives the complement
a vote majority and at most 256 seats. Throughout, $V$ includes all valid
federal-deputy votes, including votes for parties winning no seats.


Representation ratios express the same departures in relative rather than absolute terms \parencite{taagepera1980proportionality}. At the party and coalition levels,
% \[
% R_i=\frac{s_i/S}{v_i/V},
% \qquad
% R_C=\frac{s_C/S}{v_C/V}
% =\frac{s_C}{q_C}
% =1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}
% =\sum_{i\in C}\frac{q_i}{q_C}R_i.
% \]

\[
R_i=\frac{s_i/S}{v_i/V}=\frac{s_i}{q_i},
\qquad
R_C=\frac{s_C/S}{v_C/V}
=\frac{s_C}{q_C}
=1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}
=\sum_{i\in C}\frac{q_i}{q_C}R_i.
\]
A value of one denotes exact proportionality, a value above one denotes overrepresentation, and a value below one denotes underrepresentation. Parties receiving votes but no seats have \(R_i=0\).The ratios permit comparisons across parties and coalitions of different sizes. The differentials remain the more direct quantities for the majority-threshold question because they measure the relevant departure in seats.


The national coalition differential can be decomposed by adding and subtracting
the seats that exact proportionality would assign to the coalition separately
within each district:
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_C
&=
\left[
\sum_d
\left(
s_{Cd}-S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
\right)
\right]
+
\left[
\left(
\sum_d S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
\right)
-
\left(
S\frac{v_C}{V}
\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
The first term compares the coalition's actual seats in each district with the
number implied by its vote share in that district. I denote it the
within-district component, \(A_C\). The other component is the between-district
component, \(B_C\). Its first term sums the coalition's proportional quota in
each district. The second is the quota implied by the coalition's share of the
national valid vote. These quantities need not coincide because districts differ
in the number of seats they contribute relative to the number of valid votes
cast in them. Thus, \(B_C\) depends on the geographic distribution of coalition
support across districts with different electoral weights. Support concentrated
in districts whose share of Chamber seats exceeds their share of the national
valid vote raises the sum of district quotas relative to the national quota,
while support concentrated in districts with the opposite weighting lowers it.

Thus, \(d_C=A_C+B_C\).
For comparisons across coalitions of different electoral sizes, the two
components can also be expressed relative to the coalition's proportional quota:
\[
R_C-1=\frac{A_C}{q_C}+\frac{B_C}{q_C}.
\]
This expresses the within- and between-district contributions to the coalition's
relative departure from proportionality.

In Brazil, \(A_C\) can reflect the mechanisms that
operate within each district when votes are converted into seats: finite district
magnitude, the electoral-quotient and highest-averages calculations, the
year-specific eligibility rules for parties and candidates, and the pooling of
votes through proportional electoral coalitions in 2014 and 2018 and party
federations in 2022. These mechanisms determine how the coalition's actual
district seats depart from its district-level proportional quotas. By contrast,
\(B_C\) arises from differences in electoral weight across districts and the
geographic distribution of coalition support: district apportionment, turnout,
and valid-vote variation determine how many seats correspond to a given number
of votes in each district, while coalition geography determines whether its
support is concentrated in relatively more or less heavily weighted districts.
Neither component isolates the independent effect of any one mechanism; the
decomposition locates the coalition differential within or between districts
rather than providing a causal decomposition of its institutional sources
\parencite{bochsler2026winners}.


The primary ideological universe is the set of parliamentary parties,
\[
P^+=\{i\in P:s_i>0\}.
\]
National proportionality is evaluated over \(P\), while potential parliamentary
coalitions are constructed over \(P^+\). Thus, votes for parties without seats
remain part of \(V\) and of party-level proportionality accounting, but those
parties cannot constitute or interrupt a primary ideological coalition. This
restriction does not alter observed cabinet membership or its election-year
translation.

Axelrod's connected-coalition criterion provides the baseline restriction
\parencite{axelrod1970conflict}. Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) denote the existing
election-year ideological order restricted to \(P^+\), preserving the relative
ordering of represented parties without re-estimating their positions. A
coalition \(C\subseteq P^+\) is connected when its members form a contiguous
interval in this filtered order. A connected coalition is minimal winning when
\(s_C\geq257\) and no proper connected subset retains a Chamber majority.
For an interval, removing either endpoint must therefore leave fewer than
257 seats.

Exact connectedness (\(k=0\)) provides the baseline ideological domain. As a
nested sensitivity analysis, I also allow one represented party inside a
coalition's ideological span to be omitted (\(k=1\)). The formal definition and
summary results for this one-gap domain appear in Appendix~\ref{app:one-gap};
full coalition-level results are in the machine-readable replication outputs.

As a sensitivity analysis, I repeat both searches in the full election-year
order over \(P\), including parties receiving votes but no seats. In that
universe, such parties can be coalition members and can interrupt connectedness.
Admissibility and minimality are recomputed within that order. Only the
ideological universe changes: votes, seats, quotas, differentials, representation
ratios, and the decomposition retain the same definitions and national vote
denominator. Appendix~\ref{app:ideological-universes} compares the results.


\subsection{Data and operationalization}
\label{sec:data}

Vote and seat data for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 federal-deputy elections
come from the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral, accessed through the
\texttt{electionsBR} package \parencite{electionsbr}.\footnote{All 2022
results refer to the official seat vector in the frozen dataset, prior
to subsequent judicial retotalizations.} For each party, the national
vote total combines valid candidate votes and party-label votes across
the twenty-seven districts, while the seat total counts deputies elected
under its election-year label.\footnote{For 2014, the vote measure is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS + QT\_VOTOS\_LEGENDA}. For 2018 and 2022,
it is \texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS\_VALIDOS +
QT\_TOTAL\_VOTOS\_LEG\_VALIDOS}.} This attribution also applies to
parties that competed through proportional electoral coalitions in
2014 and 2018 or federations in 2022. The resulting party totals connect
the district electoral outcomes to the party identities observed in
cabinet participation and ideological classifications.

Cabinet composition is reconstructed from publicly available online records for
the Dilma Rousseff, Michel Temer, Jair Bolsonaro, and Lula III administrations,
recording each officeholder's service dates and party affiliation at the time. I
map these dated affiliations to the party labels of the election that produced
the corresponding legislative term and treat each distinct party set within that
term as one cabinet configuration. When the same configuration recurs, its dates
are retained for duration summaries but it is not counted as a new
configuration.

Each cabinet configuration is evaluated against the vote and seat distribution
of the election that produced the corresponding Chamber: 2014 for the
2015--2018 term, 2018 for 2019--2022, and 2022 for Lula III through March
19, 2026. Parties that renamed or merged during a term are matched to the
election-year parties from which their vote and seat totals derive. This yields
12 distinct cabinet configurations for the 2014 term, 18 for 2018, and 5 for
2022. Appendix~\ref{app:composition} reports their membership.

The positive within-district component of the two inversions extends to all 35
cabinet configurations: \(A_C>0\) in every case, while \(B_C>0\) in only
thirteen. Cabinet composition helps explain this regularity. In every
configuration, cabinet members have a higher mean national vote share than
parties outside the cabinet in the same election-year party system, with gaps
ranging from 1.46 to 4.95 percentage points. Larger parties are also usually on
the positive side of the within-district component: among parties receiving at
least 5 percent of the national vote, \(A_i>0\) for six of seven in 2014, eight
of nine in 2018, and all seven in 2022. In 34 of the 35 cabinet configurations,
the positive \(A_i\) contributions of members above this benchmark exceed all
negative member contributions combined; only one configuration also requires
positive contributions from smaller members to keep \(A_C>0\). Cabinet
composition therefore repeatedly brings together larger parties whose
within-district contributions are predominantly positive. No comparable sign
pattern appears for \(B_C\). of coalition support across
districts.

The two inverted cabinet party sets differ in how the positive \(A_C\)
interacts with between-district weighting. For 14-05, \(A_C=15.74\)
raises the coalition's quota of 238.73 to \(q_C+A_C=254.47\) seats,
still short of a Chamber majority. Adding \(B_C=4.53\) brings the
observed total to 259 seats. For 22-01, by contrast, \(A_C=13.20\)
already exceeds the 6.20 seats separating the quota from a majority:
\(q_C+A_C=263.99\). The negative between-district component,
\(B_C=-0.99\), reduces the total to 263 seats without removing the
majority. Thus, within-district gains characterize both inversions, while
between-district weighting reinforces the Dilma coalition and offsets the
Lula coalition. Only in the former is the positive between-district
component also needed to bridge the proportional distance to 257 seats.

components enter the differential that carries the coalition across 257.

seat differential.

representational advantage.


\section{Discussion}


The first result is a contrast between realized rarity and a broader range
of potential inversions. Among the 35 distinct cabinet party sets, only two
invert. Among the twenty minimal connected winning coalitions, seven do.
Cabinet participation therefore shows that coalition inversion was
institutionally realized, but only in a small number of the governing party
configurations actually observed. The ideological analysis shows, in turn,
that these cabinets do not exhaust the politically structured combinations
under which the same electoral distributions can reverse vote and seat
majorities. The two domains answer different questions: the cabinet analysis
identifies inversions that occurred in government, whereas the connected
coalition analysis identifies inversion possibilities among ideologically
ordered parliamentary majorities.

The relaxation of exact connectedness reinforces this interpretation. Allowing
one represented party inside an ideological span to be omitted preserves the
strongest inversions in 2014 and 2022 and reveals a stronger case in 2018, where
a coalition with 47.59 percent of the vote reaches exactly 257 seats. The
existence of potential inversions is therefore not an artifact of requiring
every intervening party to participate. At the same time, these admissible
coalitions are not predictions of coalition formation. Rarity among observed
cabinets does not establish scarcity among potential coalitions, just as the
existence of additional potential inversions does not establish their likelihood
of formation.


A second implication is that inversion is not simply a stronger form of
overrepresentation. Overrepresentation is common among cabinet coalitions: 33 of
the 35 sets have \(R_C>1\), yet only two combine a vote minority with a seat
majority. \(R_C\) measures the coalition's advantage relative to its vote share,
while \(d_C\) expresses that advantage in seats. Whether this advantage changes
majority status also depends on \(q_C\), which determines how far the coalition
lies from 257 seats under exact proportionality. A positive differential is
therefore insufficient for inversion unless it is large enough to bridge that
remaining gap. the remaining distance to a seat majority. MDB--UNI\~AO and
PP--PL illustrate the point. PP--PL is more strongly overrepresented but starts
farther below 257, so its larger differential produces the smaller seat
majority. Overrepresentation alone therefore determines neither whether a
coalition inverts nor the size of the resulting majority.



The Brazilian case adds a second stage to the aggregation problem identified
by Kurrild-Klitgaard and Miller. At the coalition level, positive and negative
party differentials reinforce or offset one another as parties are combined.
In Brazil, however, those party differentials are themselves national
aggregates of twenty-seven independently determined district outcomes. The
decomposition distinguishes the part of the coalition differential associated
with departures from proportionality within districts from the part arising
because the sum of district-level proportional quotas differs from the
coalition's national quota.  Both components carry into national party seat totals and, ultimately, into
coalition majority status.

The analysis therefore follows two successive aggregations. District outcomes
are first combined into national party seat totals, and those party-level
differentials are then recombined as different political coalitions are formed.
The \(A_C\) and \(B_C\) decomposition adds a separate accounting step by locating
the resulting coalition differential within and between districts, while the
party contributions show which members add to or offset it. Neither exercise
identifies the independent effects of district magnitude, eligibility rules,
electoral pooling, malapportionment, or the other mechanisms that contribute to
these quantities. The Brazilian extension therefore studies how the established
inversion problem operates when national representation is itself produced
through multiple district allocations and subsequently recombined at the
coalition level.


The decomposition also places the inversion cases within a broader set of
coalition configurations. This matters because some of the patterns observed
among inverted coalitions are not distinctive to inversion itself. All 35
distinct cabinet party sets have a positive within-district component,
whereas the between-district component is positive in only thirteen. The
two cabinet inversions therefore belong to a much wider pattern in which
cabinet composition repeatedly combines positive within-district
contributions, including in party sets that remain minorities in both votes
and seats or majorities in both. The party-level contributions help explain
this regularity: larger cabinet members often supply substantial positive
\(A_i\) contributions, while smaller parties can reinforce or offset them.
What distinguishes the inverted cabinet sets is consequently not the presence
of a positive \(A_C\), but the combination of these components with a
coalition quota close enough to the majority threshold for the resulting
differential to change majority status.

The ideological coalitions show that the composition of this advantage also
varies substantially among inversions themselves. Under the one-gap
relaxation, \(A_C>0\) in 97 of the 100 minimal inversions, but
\(A_C>B_C\) in only 77. In 2022, the within-district component is larger
in only five of twelve cases. A positive within-district contribution is
therefore pervasive without being universally dominant. Component size
must also be distinguished from its role in reaching the majority threshold.
A relatively small positive \(B_C\) can provide the remaining seats needed
after \(A_C\) has brought a coalition close to 257, even when \(A_C\)
accounts for most of the total differential. This is an arithmetic property
of the observed coalition decomposition rather than a counterfactual claim
about removing any particular electoral mechanism. The decomposition thus
shows that coalitions with the same inversion status, and even coalitions
with similar overall representational advantages, can occupy quite different
positions with respect to within-district allocation and between-district
weighting.

This result deepens Miller's aggregation argument in an important respect. A
fixed electoral distribution does not imply a fixed political beneficiary of
coalition inversion. The same party vote and seat totals can sustain different
majority relations across governing configurations and can place potential
inversions in different regions of the party system.The Dilma inversion ends
when the remaining cabinet parties lose their seat majority, whereas the initial
Lula inversion ends when the expanded cabinet acquires a vote majority as well.
Neither transition reflects a new electoral allocation. The same party vote and
seat totals instead support different coalition-level majority relations at
different moments of the legislative term.

The ideological results show that this variation is not only temporal. In 2022,
the realized Lula cabinet inversion is a broad, sparse, left-anchored coalition
(Figure~\ref{fig:cabinet-membership-inversions}),
while the minimal connected inversions are located on the right, including the
compact PP--PL interval. The same electoral distribution therefore supports
inversion in politically quite different parts of the coalition space. The 2018
election provides the converse pattern: no observed cabinet party set inverts,
even though an exact-connected inversion exists and the one-gap relaxation
reveals a stronger one. Party-level overrepresentation consequently does not
imply a stable advantage for one governing bloc or ideological side. Large
parties can contribute substantial positive or negative differentials, but which
political combinations benefit from them depends on how those contributions are
distributed across coalitions. The analysis identifies these representational
consequences of coalition structure; it does not imply that parties select
partners in order to exploit them.

These results concern a specific relationship between electoral outcomes and
politically relevant combinations of parties. Cabinet membership identifies
the parties represented in government, not the coalition supporting the
executive in every legislative vote. Likewise, the vote share attributed to
a cabinet or potential coalition is obtained by summing the election-year
votes of its member parties; it is not a direct electoral endorsement of the
later coalition as a collective actor. The ideological analysis imposes a
different restriction: admissible coalitions are defined relative to a fixed
estimated election-year party order, so alternative party placements can
change the boundaries of connected or one-gap coalitions. Finally, the
\(A_C+B_C\) decomposition separates the observed coalition differential
into a within-district allocation component and a between-district weighting
component. It does not identify the independent causal effects of district
magnitude, thresholds, electoral pooling, malapportionment, turnout, or the
other institutional and geographical processes that contribute to those
components.

The cabinet chronology retains the corresponding election-year vote and
seat distribution as its benchmark while cabinet membership changes over
the legislative term. It therefore tracks the representational consequences
of changing government party sets against a fixed electoral allocation,
rather than subsequent changes in parliamentary affiliation or legislative
support. These boundaries define the object of the analysis: how national
votes and realized seat totals relate to the legislative majorities available
to observed and ideologically structured combinations of parties.



\section{Conclusion}

The Brazilian case shows that coalition inversion is neither merely a
theoretical possibility nor a routine feature of coalition politics under
proportional representation. It occurs among observed governing party sets,
but rarely: only two of the 35 distinct cabinet configurations invert. The
same electoral distributions, however, permit a broader range of inversions
among ideologically structured parliamentary majorities. Seven of the twenty
minimal connected winning coalitions invert, with cases in all three
elections, and allowing a single omission inside the ideological span
expands that number to 100 of 234 minimal winning coalitions. The central
empirical result is therefore a contrast between realized rarity and a wider
set of politically structured inversion possibilities.

The analysis also clarifies what makes these cases institutionally
consequential. Coalition inversion is not simply another name for
overrepresentation: it occurs when the accumulated party-level seat
differential is large enough to change majority status. The multidistrict
decomposition then distinguishes coalitions whose aggregate advantage is
similar in size but composed differently across within-district allocation
and between-district weighting. This comparison also reveals a broader
pattern beyond the inverted cases themselves. Every observed cabinet party
set has a positive within-district component, whereas positive
between-district contributions are much less systematic. The inversion cases
can therefore be situated within a wider structure of coalition
representation, showing both what they share with other party combinations
and what makes their accumulated advantage sufficient to alter the
vote-majority and seat-majority relation.

Proportional representation is usually described through the relationship
between each party's votes and seats. Once those allocations are combined
into parliamentary majorities, however, an additional structure becomes
visible. Coalition inversion identifies the configurations in which ordinary
party-level deviations are sufficient to reverse the relation between vote
majorities and seat majorities. This structure makes it possible to ask where
such reversals lie within a party system, whether they are realized in
governing coalitions, and how their incidence changes as the same legislature
is recombined. At the same time, the relation being reversed is a basic one
for democratic representation: a set of parties supported by less than half
of the national vote can control a majority of legislative seats. Coalition
inversions therefore reveal both an analytical property of proportional
systems and a majoritarian tension within the parliamentary outcomes those
systems can produce.

\subsection{Admissible domains and the one-gap relaxation}
\label{app:one-gap}

Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) order the parties in \(P^*\), where \(P^*=P^+\)
contains seat-winning parties in the primary specification and \(P^*=P\)
contains all parties in the sensitivity analysis. For nonempty
\(C\subseteq P^*\), let \(a=\min\{j:p_j\in C\}\) and
\(b=\max\{j:p_j\in C\}\). Define
\[
\operatorname{span}(C)=\{p_a,\ldots,p_b\},\qquad
 g(C)=|\operatorname{span}(C)\setminus C|,\qquad
 \mathcal D_k=\{\varnothing\ne C\subseteq P^*:g(C)\leq k\}.
\]
Thus, \(k=0\) requires connectedness, while \(k=1\) permits at most one
omitted party inside the span. A winning \(C\) is minimal only if no proper
subset admissible in the same \(\mathcal D_k\) retains at least 257 seats.
Minimality is recomputed for each \(k\) and party universe: nested admissible
domains do not imply nested collections of minimal winning coalitions.

All twenty baseline minimal majorities remain admissible and winning under
\(k=1\), but only six remain minimal. Table~\ref{tab:one-gap-summary}
compares the counts. The primary one-gap domain contains 100 inversions among
234 minimal winning coalitions. Full enumeration, memberships, omissions, and
decompositions for both universes remain in the machine-readable replication
outputs.
